A letter from the building department, an open permit from a project that never got its final inspection, or a finished basement nobody ever filed paperwork for: code problems are one of the most common reasons Orange County homeowners feel stuck. The good news is that a house with code violations can still be sold. The real question is who fixes the problem, who pays for it, and how much time you have.

This guide walks through how code issues usually surface in a sale, what your options are in places like [Newburgh](/sell-my-house-fast-newburgh-ny), [Middletown](/sell-my-house-fast-middletown-ny), [Goshen](/sell-my-house-fast-goshen-ny), [Monroe](/sell-my-house-fast-monroe-ny), [Warwick](/sell-my-house-fast-warwick-ny), and [Cornwall](/sell-my-house-fast-cornwall-ny), and how a cash, as-is sale handles them. Every municipality runs its own building department and procedures, so treat this as a starting point and confirm the details for your parcel with your attorney and the local code office.

## How code enforcement works in Orange County

New York State has a statewide building and fire code, but enforcement is mostly local. In Orange County that means the city, town, or village where the house sits—its building department or code enforcement officer—handles permits, inspections, violation notices, and certificates of occupancy. The City of Newburgh, the City of Middletown, and the City of Port Jervis each run their own offices, as do the county's towns and villages.

Because enforcement is local, the same problem can play out differently from one municipality to the next. Inspection schedules, re-inspection fees, rental registration rules, and how violations are recorded can all vary. Ask the building department that covers your address for its records on the property, and ask what it would take to close each open item.

## Common code problems we see on older Hudson Valley homes

Orange County has a lot of older housing stock, and much of it has been updated piece by piece over decades. The issues that tend to come up include:

* **Open or expired permits.** A roof, deck, furnace, or addition was permitted but never got a final inspection, so the permit is still open on the town's books.
* **Work done without a permit.** Finished basements, added bathrooms, enclosed porches, or extra apartments that were never approved.
* **Missing or outdated certificate of occupancy.** The house's legal use or layout does not match what is actually there—for example, a single-family home being used as a two-family.
* **Property maintenance violations.** Unsafe porches or stairs, roof leaks, broken windows, overgrown yards, debris, or a vacant building that is not secured.
* **Safety items.** Missing smoke or carbon monoxide alarms, electrical hazards, or heating systems that need repair.
* **Unpaid fines or municipal charges.** Some municipalities can add unpaid charges to the tax bill, which then shows up during the title search.

## How code issues show up in a sale

Many sellers assume a buyer will never find out. In practice, code issues usually surface during the deal. The buyer's attorney and title company typically run municipal searches along with the title search, and those can turn up open permits, violations, or certificate-of-occupancy gaps. A home inspector may also spot unpermitted work.

In a traditional listed sale, that discovery often lands late. A lender may not want to finance a property with open violations or an illegal unit, and the buyer may ask you to close out permits, make repairs, or give a credit before closing. That can mean hiring contractors, scheduling inspections, and waiting on the building department while your closing date slides.

## Your three main options

**1. Fix the violations and then sell.** If the issues are small and you have the time and cash, curing them can make the house easier to list. That usually means contractors, permit applications, inspections, and sign-off from the building department. It is the right path for some sellers, but costs and timelines are hard to predict until the work is opened up.

**2. List it as-is with an agent.** You can list the property and disclose the issues. Expect fewer financed buyers, more negotiation, and possibly a buyer who still asks for repairs or credits after their inspection.

**3. Sell as-is to a cash buyer.** A cash buyer who purchases as-is takes on the code problems after closing. There is no lender to object to open permits or an unapproved unit, and the price reflects the condition up front instead of being renegotiated later. You can read more about how this works on our [selling a house as-is](/situations/as-is) page.

None of these options removes your attorney's role. Whatever path you choose, your attorney will review the contract, the municipal search results, and how any open items or unpaid charges get handled at closing.

## Practical tips if your house has code issues

* **Get the records.** Ask your city, town, or village building department for open permits, violations, and the certificate of occupancy on file.
* **Keep every letter.** Violation notices, compliance deadlines, and inspection reports help your attorney and any buyer understand the situation.
* **Don't ignore deadlines.** Contact the code office if you need more time; ignoring a notice can lead to added fines.
* **Disclose what you know.** Being upfront protects you and keeps the deal from falling apart late.
* **Talk to your attorney before signing anything.** Make sure the contract clearly states the sale is as-is and how open items will be handled.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I sell a house with open code violations in New York?

Yes. Code violations don't legally prevent a sale, but they usually come up in the buyer's municipal search and can limit financing. Many sellers either fix the issues first or sell as-is to a cash buyer who takes them on. Your attorney can explain how open items will be handled in your contract.

### Do I have to fix code violations before selling my house in Orange County?

Not necessarily. A buyer purchasing as-is can agree to take the property with open violations. Some financed buyers or their lenders will require repairs first, which is why sellers with significant code issues often look at cash offers.

### What is an open permit and why does it matter?

An open permit is a building permit that was issued but never closed with a final inspection. It can show up in the buyer's municipal search and may need to be closed out, or the buyer may agree to take responsibility for it, before or after closing.

### What if my house doesn't have a certificate of occupancy?

A missing or outdated certificate of occupancy can complicate a financed sale, especially when the house's use or layout has changed. Ask your local building department what is on file. A cash buyer purchasing as-is may accept the property in its current state.

### Will unpaid code fines show up at closing?

They can. Some municipalities add unpaid fines or charges to the property tax bill or record them against the property, and the title search may turn them up. Your attorney and the title company will identify what must be paid at closing.
